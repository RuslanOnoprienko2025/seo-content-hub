Getting that right matters more when you're the one managing communication on someone else's behalf.

## What to Include in an Assistant Email Signature

Every email signature needs six essential elements.

**1. Full Name**

Use your own name, not the executive's. Even when you're writing on someone else's behalf, the signature identifies the sender.

**2. Professional Title**

Be specific. "Executive Assistant to the CEO" means something different to a recipient than "Administrative Assistant." The title sets context for the recipient and helps explain the role you're communicating in.

**3. Company**

The organization you represent. It's often the most recognizable part of the signature, especially when recipients don't know you personally.

**4. Phone Number**

Your direct line or the main office number—whichever is the right number for external contacts to reach you. Not a personal mobile unless that's genuinely how you want to be reached for work.

**5. Email Address**

Your work email. It travels with forwarded messages, printed threads, and shared correspondence—putting it in the signature means it's always accessible regardless of context.

**6. Company Website**

The organization's website. A useful reference for recipients who want to learn more about the company without asking for additional information.

## Executive Assistant Email Signature Examples

These executive assistant email signature examples demonstrate proper formatting with all six essential fields included.

### Example 1: 

```
Sarah Mitchell
Executive Assistant
Acme Corporation
(512) 555-0123
sarah.mitchell@acmecorp.com
acmecorp.com
```

**Why it works:** Clean and direct. The title makes the role immediately clear without requiring explanation. External contacts know they're dealing with someone who manages executive-level communication, not a general inbox.

### Example 2: 

```
James Wilson
Executive Assistant to the CEO
Northfield Group
(312) 555-4567
james.wilson@northfieldgroup.com
northfieldgroup.com
```

**Why it works:** "Executive Assistant to the CEO" tells the recipient exactly where this person sits in the organization. For vendors, clients, and external partners, that context changes how they read the message. It signals that responses should be timely and taken seriously.

Rodriguez Consulting

**Why it works:** Personal assistant roles vary widely—some are purely administrative, others handle a broader range of responsibilities. Keeping the title straightforward and letting the company name carry the organizational context works well here.

## Should You Include the Executive's Name?

This comes up often for assistants—whether to add the executive's name somewhere in the signature.

The short answer: usually not.

The signature identifies who sent the message. If you're Elena Rodriguez, Personal Assistant at Rodriguez Consulting, that's what the signature says. Recipients who receive regular communication from assistants understand the dynamic without needing it spelled out.

Where it does make sense is in the job title itself. "Executive Assistant to Sarah Chen, Managing Director" tells the recipient something useful—specifically, whose schedule, correspondence, and priorities you're managing. It signals that you work closely with the organization's decision-maker.

What doesn't work: listing both your name and the executive's name as if you're co-signing. It creates confusion about who the actual contact is. If a recipient wants to reach the CEO directly, adding the CEO's name to your signature doesn't help them do that. It just clutters the block.

Keep the signature in your name. If the executive's identity is relevant, put it in the title.
